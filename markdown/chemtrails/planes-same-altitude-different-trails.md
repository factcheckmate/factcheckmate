Planes appearing at the same altitude can actually be separated by 1,000+ feet—enough for dramatically different contrail formation. Humid air layers that cause persistent contrails are known to be wide but shallow. A difference of just 1,000 feet means one aircraft produces a contrail while another doesn't. Additionally, newer, more efficient engines produce more water vapor and cooler exhaust, causing contrails at lower altitudes than older engines. German research showed an Airbus A340 (1991) leaving contrails at 33,000 feet while a Boeing 707 (1957) at the same altitude produced none—purely due to engine efficiency differences. From the ground, accurately judging aircraft altitude is nearly impossible without specialized equipment.
